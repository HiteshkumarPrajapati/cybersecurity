## 1. Core GRC & Risk Frameworks

### 1.1 ISO 31000:2018 – Risk Management

**What:** International standard providing principles, framework, and process for managing risk across any organization, regardless of size, activity, or sector. [web:50][web:53]

**Why:** Helps organizations increase the likelihood of achieving objectives, improve identification of opportunities and threats, and effectively allocate resources for risk treatment. [web:50]

**When to Use:**
- Establishing or improving enterprise risk management (ERM) programs
- Integrating risk management into governance, strategy, planning, and operations
- When stakeholders require internationally-recognized risk management practices [web:56]

**Where to Use:**
- All organizational levels (board, executive, operational)
- All functions: IT, cybersecurity, compliance, quality, health & safety, business continuity [web:56]
- Heavily regulated industries: financial services, healthcare, energy, government

**Brief Description:**
ISO 31000 defines risk as "the effect of uncertainty on objectives" and provides a flexible, principles-based approach rather than prescriptive requirements. [web:53][web:56] It emphasizes leadership commitment, integration into organizational activities, and continual improvement. [web:56]

**Objectives:**
- Create and protect organizational value
- Improve performance and encourage innovation
- Support achievement of objectives
- Improve identification of opportunities and threats
- Comply with legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements [web:50]

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Foundation for integrating cybersecurity risk into enterprise risk management
- Provides common risk language for board and executive reporting
- Enables risk-based prioritization of security controls and investments
- Not certifiable, but forms basis for audit-ready risk programs [web:50][web:56]

**Best Practices:**
- Tailor the framework to organizational context, culture, and objectives [web:53]
- Ensure top management ownership and visible commitment [web:56]
- Embed risk management into decision-making at all levels
- Use iterative approach: continually update based on new knowledge and experience [web:56]
- Integrate with other management systems (ISO 27001, ISO 22301, COSO)

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Risk registers and heat maps
- Scenario analysis and stress testing
- Bow-tie analysis for critical risks
- Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) and dashboards
- Risk assessment workshops and interviews

**Key Control Activities:**
- Establish risk management policy and commitment
- Define risk criteria (appetite, tolerance, thresholds)
- Implement risk assessment process (identification, analysis, evaluation)
- Treat risks via avoidance, reduction, sharing, or acceptance
- Monitor, review, and communicate risks continuously

### 1.2 AS 3806:2006 – Compliance Programs

**What:** Australian Standard providing guidance on principles for effective management of an organization's compliance with legal obligations and other relevant obligations. [web:54]

**Why:** Ensures organizations can demonstrate commitment to compliance, manage compliance obligations systematically, and achieve continual improvement in compliance performance. [web:54]

**When to Use:**
- Designing or enhancing compliance management systems (CMS)
- Responding to regulatory expectations for compliance programs
- When seeking to demonstrate compliance culture to regulators (e.g., ASIC, APRA)

**Where to Use:**
- All regulated industries in Australia (financial services, healthcare, AML/CTF)
- Organizations with significant legal, regulatory, or contractual obligations
- Boards and senior management overseeing compliance risk

**Brief Description:**
AS 3806 covers commitment to compliance, implementation of compliance programs, monitoring and measuring compliance, and continual improvement. [web:54] It aligns with ISO 19600 (Compliance Management Systems) principles.

**Objectives:**
- Establish compliance commitment from leadership
- Identify and manage compliance obligations
- Implement effective compliance controls
- Monitor, measure, and report compliance performance
- Continually improve compliance management

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Cybersecurity and privacy are key compliance obligations under multiple Australian laws
- Provides framework for demonstrating "reasonable steps" to regulators after incidents
- Supports board assurance on cyber and data protection compliance
- Integrates with ISO 31000 risk management and ISO 27001 security controls

**Best Practices:**
- Obtain explicit board and executive commitment to compliance
- Maintain compliance obligation register (laws, regulations, contracts, standards)
- Assign clear compliance roles and responsibilities
- Conduct regular compliance training and awareness
- Implement compliance monitoring, auditing, and reporting

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Compliance obligation registers and matrices
- Compliance risk assessments
- Compliance training platforms (LMS)
- Compliance monitoring and audit programs
- Breach and incident management workflows

**Key Control Activities:**
- Compliance policy and code of conduct
- Compliance risk assessment and treatment
- Compliance training and communication
- Compliance monitoring, auditing, and reporting
- Breach management and remediation

### 1.3 OCEG GRC Capability Model (Red Book)

**What:** Open Compliance & Ethics Group (OCEG) GRC Capability Model providing comprehensive framework for building organizational GRC capability. [web:18]

**Why:** Enables organizations to integrate governance, risk management, and compliance activities into a unified capability, reducing silos and improving efficiency.

**When to Use:**
- Designing or maturing integrated GRC programs
- When GRC activities are fragmented across functions
- Seeking holistic view of GRC capabilities and gaps

**Where to Use:**
- Enterprise-wide GRC programs
- Organizations with complex regulatory environments
- GRC technology selection and implementation

**Brief Description:**
The Red Book defines GRC capabilities across four components:
- **Learn:** Understand context, obligations, and risks
- **Align:** Set strategy, objectives, and policies
- **Perform:** Execute plans, processes, and controls
- **Review:** Monitor, audit, and improve

It includes detailed capability requirements for responsive actions, controls, internal investigation, third-party management, and more. [web:18]

**Objectives:**
- Create integrated GRC capability across the organization
- Reduce duplication and inefficiency in GRC activities
- Improve GRC decision-making and performance
- Enable scalable, adaptive GRC programs

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Provides blueprint for integrating cyber risk, security, privacy, and compliance
- Supports design of GRC operating model and governance structures
- Helps map GRC capabilities to technology requirements
- Enables benchmarking and maturity assessment of GRC programs

**Best Practices:**
- Map current GRC capabilities against the model to identify gaps
- Prioritize capability improvements based on risk and value
- Integrate GRC data and processes across functions
- Use common GRC language and taxonomy
- Leverage GRC technology to enable capability integration

**Tools & Techniques:**
- GRC capability maturity assessments
- GRC process maps and workflows
- Integrated GRC platforms (e.g., ServiceNow GRC, MetricStream, OneTrust)
- GRC capability dashboards and reporting

**Key Control Activities:**
- GRC governance and oversight structures
- Integrated risk and compliance assessments
- Unified policies, standards, and controls
- Consolidated GRC monitoring and reporting
- Continuous improvement mechanisms

### 1.4 COBIT 2019 – Governance and Management of Enterprise IT

**What:** ISACA's framework for governance and management of enterprise IT, providing objectives, practices, and activities for IT governance and management. [web:57]

**Why:** Helps organizations create optimal value from IT by maintaining balance between realizing benefits and optimizing risk levels and resource use.

- Designing or improving IT governance frameworks
- Aligning IT with business objectives and risk appetite
- When seeking comprehensive IT governance and management practices

**Where to Use:**
- IT governance and management functions
- Enterprise architecture and IT strategy
- IT risk management and compliance programs

**Brief Description:**
COBIT 2019 provides:
- **Governance objectives** (EDM: Evaluate, Direct, Monitor)
- **Management objectives** (APO, BAI, DSS, MEA domains)
- Design factors to tailor the framework to organizational context
- Performance management and maturity models

It includes 40 governance and management objectives with associated practices and activities. [web:57]

**Objectives:**
- Ensure stakeholder needs are understood and addressed
- Optimize risk and resource allocation for IT
- Enable IT to support business strategy and objectives
- Provide comprehensive IT governance and management practices
- Support compliance with regulatory and standards requirements

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Provides IT governance framework that integrates cybersecurity and risk management
- Supports alignment of security investments with business value and risk
- Enables comprehensive IT control environment design
- Complements ISO 27001, NIST, and other security frameworks

**Best Practices:**
- Tailor COBIT objectives to organizational context using design factors
- Integrate with enterprise risk management (ISO 31000)
- Map COBIT practices to other frameworks (ISO 27001, NIST CSF, CIS Controls)
- Use COBIT for IT governance maturity assessments
- Focus on outcomes and value delivery, not just processes

**Tools & Techniques:**
- COBIT governance and management objective assessments
- IT governance maturity models
- Process capability assessments (CMMI-based)
- IT governance dashboards and performance metrics

**Key Control Activities (Selected):**
- **EDM03:** Ensure risk optimization
- **APO12:** Manage risk
- **APO13:** Manage security
- **DSS05:** Manage security services
- **MEA03:** Monitor, evaluate, and assess compliance

## 2. Information Security & Business Continuity

### 2.1 ISO/IEC 27001:2022 – Information Security Management Systems (ISMS)

**What:** International standard specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS). [web:72]

**Why:** Provides systematic approach to managing sensitive company information, ensuring its confidentiality, integrity, and availability through risk management.

**When to Use:**
- When seeking certifiable information security management system
- Customer or regulatory requirements for ISO 27001 certification
- Establishing comprehensive information security governance

**Where to Use:**
- All organizations handling sensitive information
- Industries with high security requirements: finance, healthcare, technology, government
- Supply chain security requirements (vendors must be certified)

**Brief Description:**
ISO 27001 follows Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle and requires organizations to:
- Define scope and context of ISMS
- Establish information security policy and objectives
- Conduct risk assessment and treatment
- Implement controls from Annex A (93 controls in 4 themes)
- Monitor, measure, audit, and improve ISMS

**Objectives:**
- Protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information
- Manage information security risks systematically
- Demonstrate compliance to stakeholders
- Achieve continuous improvement in security posture
- Enable certifiable security management system

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Most widely recognized and certifiable security standard globally
- Provides comprehensive control set (Annex A) for security programs
- Enables vendor security assessments and third-party audits
- Foundation for integrated GRC programs (security, privacy, continuity)
- Highly valued in Australian market, especially for government and enterprise

**Best Practices:**
- Engage leadership and obtain management commitment
- Base scope on business context, not just technology
- Conduct thorough risk assessment before selecting controls
- Implement Statement of Applicability (SoA) with justifications
- Integrate with other management systems (ISO 9001, ISO 22301, GDPR)
- Prepare for surveillance audits and recertification (3-year cycle)

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Risk assessment tools (risk registers, heat maps)
- ISMS documentation platforms
- Control testing and audit management tools
- Security awareness training platforms
- Asset management and classification tools

**Key Controls (Annex A - 2022 Version, 4 Themes):**
- **Organizational (A.5):** Policies, roles, access control, supplier relationships
- **People (A.6):** Screening, training, disciplinary process, remote working
- **Physical (A.7):** Perimeter security, physical entry, equipment security
- **Technological (A.8):** Endpoint security, authentication, logging, cryptography

### 2.2 ISO/IEC 27002:2022 – Information Security Controls

**What:** Provides implementation guidance for information security controls listed in ISO 27001 Annex A, with detailed control descriptions and implementation guidance.

**Why:** Helps organizations understand how to implement and operate specific security controls effectively, going beyond the requirements in ISO 27001.

**When to Use:**
- Implementing ISO 27001 controls
- Designing security control frameworks without certification
- When detailed control implementation guidance is needed

**Where to Use:**
- Security operations and control implementation
- IT and security teams responsible for control deployment
- Security architects and engineers

**Brief Description:**
ISO 27002 provides for each control:
- Purpose and main objectives
- Implementation guidance
- Additional information and references
- It organizes 93 controls into 4 themes (Organizational, People, Physical, Technological)

**Objectives:**
- Provide detailed guidance for implementing ISO 27001 controls
- Enable organizations to select appropriate controls based on risk
- Support consistent control implementation across the organization
- Improve overall information security posture

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Essential reference for designing and assessing security controls
- Supports control testing and audit activities
- Enables gap analysis and maturity assessments
- Provides best practice guidance for security operations

**Best Practices:**
- Use in conjunction with ISO 27001 for comprehensive approach
- Tailor controls to organizational context and risk profile
- Document control implementation decisions and justifications
- Map controls to other frameworks (NIST, CIS, PCI DSS)
- Regularly review and update controls based on emerging threats

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Control implementation checklists
- Security architecture and design patterns
- Configuration management and hardening guides
- Control testing and validation procedures

### 2.3 ISO/IEC 27701:2019 – Privacy Information Management

**What:** Extension to ISO 27001/27002 providing requirements and guidance for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a Privacy Information Management System (PIMS).

**Why:** Helps organizations manage privacy obligations systematically, demonstrating compliance with privacy regulations (GDPR, APPs, etc.) through integrated security and privacy management.

**When to Use:**
- When seeking to demonstrate privacy compliance systematically
- Implementing privacy management alongside ISO 27001
- Customer or regulatory requirements for privacy certifications

**Where to Use:**
- Organizations processing personal data
- Privacy compliance programs (GDPR, APPs, CCPA, etc.)
- Data protection and privacy operations

**Brief Description:**
ISO 27701 extends ISO 27001/27002 by:
- Adding privacy-specific requirements to ISMS
- Providing controls for protecting personally identifiable information (PII)
- Addressing PII controller and PII processor requirements
- Supporting privacy compliance across multiple jurisdictions

**Objectives:**
- Protect PII through integrated security and privacy management
- Demonstrate compliance with privacy regulations
- Reduce privacy risks and breaches
- Build trust with customers and stakeholders regarding privacy

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Bridges security (ISO 27001) and privacy compliance (Privacy Act, GDPR)
- Enables certifiable privacy management system
- Supports Privacy Act 1988 and APP compliance in Australia
- Valuable for organizations with global privacy obligations

**Best Practices:**
- Integrate PIMS with existing ISMS (ISO 27001)
- Map privacy controls to applicable regulations (APPs, GDPR Articles)
- Conduct privacy impact assessments (PIAs) for high-risk processing
- Maintain records of processing activities (RoPA)
- Implement privacy by design and default principles

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Privacy impact assessment (PIA) tools
- Records of processing activities (RoPA) registers
- Data mapping and flow diagramming
- Consent management platforms
- Data subject request (DSR) management workflows

**Key Controls (Privacy-Specific Additions):**
- PII processing transparency and notice
- Consent management and withdrawal
- Data subject rights (access, correction, deletion)
- PII breach notification and response
- PII processor agreements and oversight

### 2.4 ISO 22301:2019 – Business Continuity Management Systems (BCMS)

**What:** International standard specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a Business Continuity Management System (BCMS).

**Why:** Enables organizations to prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptive incidents, ensuring continuity of critical operations and services.

**When to Use:**
- When seeking certifiable business continuity management system
- Regulatory requirements for business continuity (APRA CPS 230, etc.)
- After experiencing significant disruptions or incidents

**Where to Use:**
- Critical infrastructure and essential services
- Financial services, healthcare, government
- Organizations with high availability requirements

**Brief Description:**
ISO 22301 follows PDCA cycle and requires organizations to:
- Understand organizational context and needs
- Establish BCMS policy and objectives
- Conduct business impact analysis (BIA) and risk assessment
- Develop business continuity strategies and plans
- Implement, test, and maintain BCMS
- Monitor, audit, and improve BCMS

**Objectives:**
- Prepare for and respond to disruptive incidents
- Minimize likelihood and impact of disruptions
- Ensure continuity of critical products and services
- Protect reputation and brand
- Demonstrate resilience to stakeholders

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Critical for cyber resilience and incident response planning
- Complements ISO 27001 (security) and ISO 31000 (risk)
- Required by APRA CPS 230 for financial entities
- Supports operational resilience expectations from regulators

**Best Practices:**
- Conduct thorough BIA to identify critical activities and RTO/RPO
- Align with enterprise risk management (ISO 31000)
- Integrate with incident management and crisis response
- Test and exercise business continuity plans regularly
- Maintain mutual alignment with ISO 27001 ISMS

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Business Impact Analysis (BIA) tools and templates
- Risk assessment and scenario analysis
- Business continuity planning software
- Crisis management and communication platforms
- Testing and exercise management tools

**Key Control Activities:**
- Business impact analysis (BIA) and risk assessment
- Business continuity strategy and solutions
- Emergency response and crisis management
- Business continuity plans and procedures
- Awareness, training, and testing programs

## 3. Australian Laws & Cross-Sector Obligations

### 3.1 Corporations Act 2001 (Cth)

**What:** Primary legislation governing companies in Australia, including duties of directors and officers, corporate governance, and disclosure obligations. [web:35]

**Why:** Establishes legal framework for corporate governance, director duties, and accountability, including cybersecurity as part of directors' duty of care and diligence.

**When to Use:**
- Advising boards and directors on governance obligations
- Designing corporate governance frameworks
- Responding to cyber incidents with board-level impact

**Where to Use:**
- All companies registered in Australia
- Board and executive governance
- Corporate disclosures and reporting

**Brief Description:**
Key sections relevant to cyber GRC:
- **Section 180(1):** Directors must exercise care and diligence (reasonable person standard)
- **Section 181:** Directors must act in good faith in best interests of company
- **Section 184:** Criminal penalties for reckless or intentionally dishonest breaches
- ASIC enforcement and penalty provisions (up to $1.65M for individuals, 15 years imprisonment) [web:35]

**Objectives:**
- Ensure proper corporate governance and accountability
- Protect shareholders and stakeholders
- Maintain market integrity and confidence
- Enable enforcement of director and officer duties

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Cybersecurity is now clearly within directors' duty of care and diligence [web:35]
- ASIC Chair has stated cyber attacks are "foreseeable risk" requiring board priority [web:35]
- Directors can be personally liable for cyber governance failures
- Supports business case for investment in cyber resilience
- Required for board reporting and governance assurance

**Best Practices:**
- Ensure board receives regular cyber risk and resilience reporting
- Document board consideration of cyber risks and mitigation strategies
- Implement cyber governance framework with clear accountability
- Conduct cyber tabletop exercises with board participation
- Maintain D&O insurance (but understand cyber exclusions) [web:35]

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Board cyber risk dashboards and reporting
- Director and officer cyber awareness training
- Cyber governance maturity assessments
- Board cyber tabletop exercises and scenarios

**Key Control Activities:**
- Board oversight of cyber risk and strategy
- Cyber risk incorporated into enterprise risk management
- Executive accountability for cyber performance
- Regular cyber incident reporting to board
- Documentation of board cyber governance decisions

### 3.2 Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) & Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)

**What:** Primary privacy legislation in Australia regulating how APP entities (organizations and agencies) collect, hold, use, disclose, and secure personal information. [web:59][web:65]

**Why:** Protects individuals' privacy rights and imposes obligations on organizations to handle personal information responsibly and securely.

**When to Use:**
- When handling personal information of Australian individuals
- Designing privacy compliance programs
- Responding to privacy complaints or breaches

**Where to Use:**
- All APP entities (organizations with turnover >$3M, plus specified entities)
- Any handling of personal information (customers, employees, etc.)
- Cross-border data transfers and disclosures

**Brief Description:**
The Privacy Act includes 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs):
- **APP 1:** Open and transparent management of personal information
- **APP 2:** Anonymity and pseudonymity
- **APP 3:** Collection of solicited personal information
- **APP 4:** Dealing with unsolicited personal information
- **APP 5:** Notification of collection
- **APP 6:** Use and disclosure of personal information
- **APP 7:** Direct marketing
- **APP 8:** Cross-border disclosure
- **APP 9:** Adoption, use, or disclosure of government identifiers
- **APP 10:** Quality of personal information
- **APP 11:** Security of personal information (critical for cyber GRC)
- **APP 12:** Access to personal information
- **APP 13:** Correction of personal information

**Objectives:**
- Protect privacy of individuals regarding personal information
- Balance privacy rights with legitimate business needs
- Enable free flow of information while ensuring accountability
- Provide redress for privacy breaches

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- APP 11 requires "reasonable steps" to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, unauthorized access, modification, or disclosure
- Privacy breaches can trigger Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme obligations
- OAIC can impose significant penalties (up to $50M+ for serious breaches)
- Foundation for privacy compliance programs and PIMS (ISO 27701)
- Critical for cyber incident response (data breaches)

**Best Practices:**
- Maintain up-to-date privacy policy (APP 1)
- Conduct privacy impact assessments for high-risk projects
- Implement data mapping and records of processing
- Apply data minimization and retention limits
- Secure personal information per APP 11 (encryption, access controls, etc.)
- Prepare for data subject rights requests (access, correction)

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Privacy impact assessment (PIA) tools
- Data mapping and classification tools
- Consent management platforms
- Data subject request (DSR) workflows
- Privacy breach detection and response tools

**Key Control Activities:**
- Privacy policy and notice management
- Consent capture and management
- Data subject rights fulfillment
- Personal information security controls (encryption, access, etc.)
- Privacy breach detection, response, and notification

### 3.3 Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 (POLA Act)

**What:** Major privacy reform legislation amending Privacy Act 1988, introducing strengthened obligations, higher penalties, and new transparency requirements including for automated decision-making. [web:58][web:62][web:64]

**Why:** Responds to Privacy Act Review 2023, enhancing privacy protections, strengthening enforcement, and addressing emerging technology risks (AI, automated decisions).

**When to Use:**
- Updating privacy compliance programs for new obligations
- Implementing automated decision-making transparency (effective Dec 2026)
- Preparing for enhanced OAIC enforcement powers

**Where to Use:**
- All APP entities subject to Privacy Act
- Organizations using AI or automated decision-making with personal information
- Privacy and legal compliance functions

**Brief Description:**
Key reforms include:
- **Increased penalties:** Up to $50M, 3x benefit gained, or 30% of turnover for serious breaches [web:64]
- **Automated decision-making transparency:** New APP 1.7-1.8 requiring disclosure in privacy policy (effective 10 Dec 2026) [web:59]
- **Enhanced OAIC powers:** Expanded investigation and enforcement capabilities
- **Children's Online Privacy Code:** New code for protecting children's privacy online
- **Statutory tort for privacy:** New cause of action for serious invasions of privacy
- **Criminal offences for doxxing:** New offences for malicious data release [web:64]

**Objectives:**
- Strengthen privacy protections for Australians
- Enhance regulatory enforcement and deterrence
- Address technology-driven privacy risks (AI, automation)
- Harmonize with international privacy standards (GDPR, etc.)

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Automated decision-making transparency creates new disclosure obligations for AI systems using personal information
- Significantly higher penalties increase business risk and board attention
- OAIC compliance sweeps targeting privacy policy compliance (including automated decisions) [web:59]
- Supports AI governance frameworks (ISO 42001, NIST AI RMF) with privacy requirements
- Creates urgency for privacy program maturity and compliance

**Best Practices:**
- Inventory automated decision-making systems using personal information
- Update privacy policies to disclose automated decision-making (by Dec 2026)
- Document kinds of personal information used, decisions made, and functions performed [web:59]
- Conduct privacy impact assessments for AI and automated systems
- Implement privacy-by-design for AI and automated decision technologies

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Automated decision system inventories
- Privacy policy management and disclosure tools
- AI and algorithmic impact assessments
- Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) for AI systems

**Key Control Activities:**
- Automated decision-making transparency in privacy policy
- AI system privacy impact assessments
- Enhanced data protection for high-risk processing
- Privacy breach detection and notification (aligned with NDB)
- Staff training on new privacy obligations and penalties

### 3.4 Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) Scheme

**What:** Mandatory data breach notification scheme under Privacy Act 1988 requiring APP entities to notify OAIC and affected individuals of eligible data breaches. [web:65][web:81]

**Why:** Enables individuals to take steps to protect their information after serious data breaches and improves organizational accountability for data security.

**When to Use:**
- When experiencing or suspecting a data breach
- Assessing whether breach is "eligible" (notifiable)
- Preparing breach response and notification

**Where to Use:**
- All APP entities subject to Privacy Act
- Incident response and breach management
- Customer and stakeholder communications

**Brief Description:**
An "eligible data breach" occurs when:
- Unauthorized access, disclosure, or loss of personal information
- Likely to result in serious harm to individuals
- Entity has not prevented risk via remedial action

**Notification requirements:**
- Notify OAIC as soon as practicable (use NDB form)
- Notify affected individuals (email, phone, website, etc.)
- Notification must include: entity details, description of breach, kinds of information, recommendations for individuals [web:65][web:81]

**Objectives:**
- Enable individuals to protect themselves after serious breaches
- Improve organizational data security practices
- Increase transparency and accountability for breaches
- Support OAIC oversight and enforcement

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Critical component of privacy incident response
- Triggers significant reputational and regulatory risk
- Requires cross-functional coordination (legal, IT, security, communications)
- OAIC publishes breach statistics and trends (benchmarking)
- Non-compliance can result in enforcement action and penalties

**Best Practices:**
- Establish data breach response plan and team
- Implement breach detection and assessment processes
- Prepare NDB notification templates and workflows
- Conduct tabletop exercises for breach scenarios
- Document all breach assessments and decisions (even if not notifiable)

**Tools & Techniques:**
- Data breach detection and monitoring tools
- Breach assessment and decision workflows
- NDB notification templates and processes
- Breach register and reporting dashboards
- Customer communication platforms

**Key Control Activities:**
- Breach detection and identification
- Breach assessment (eligibility, serious harm)
- OAIC and individual notification
- Remediation and containment actions
- Post-incident review and improvement

### 3.5 Work Health and Safety (WHS) Laws

**What:** State and territory legislation (harmonized across most jurisdictions) requiring employers to ensure health and safety of workers and others, including psychological safety and protection from harassment.

**Why:** Creates legal duty for employers to provide safe working environment, which increasingly includes cyber-related risks (online harassment, psychological safety, remote work security).

**When to Use:**
- Designing workplace safety programs
- Addressing psychosocial hazards (including cyber-related)
- Remote work and flexible work arrangements

**Where to Use:**
- All employers and workplaces in Australia
- HR and workplace safety functions
- Remote and hybrid work environments

**Brief Description:**
WHS laws impose primary duty on employers to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable:
- Health and safety of workers (physical and psychological)
- Health and safety of others (customers, visitors) influenced by work
- This includes managing psychosocial hazards (stress, bullying, harassment, including online/cyber)

**Objectives:**
- Protect worker health and safety (physical and psychological)
- Promote continuous improvement in WHS
- Provide framework for consultation and cooperation
- Enable enforcement of WHS obligations

**Importance for Cyber GRC Consultant:**
- Cyber incidents can create psychosocial hazards (stress, trauma from breaches)
- Online harassment and doxxing fall under WHS (psychological safety)
- Remote work security impacts WHS obligations (secure home environments)
- Overlaps with privacy and security obligations for employee data
- Supports holistic approach to workplace safety including digital risks

**Best Practices:**
- Include cyber-related psychosocial hazards in WHS risk assessments
- Implement secure remote work policies and technologies
- Address online harassment and doxxing in WHS policies
- Provide cyber safety training for employees
- Coordinate with privacy and security teams on employee data protection

**Tools & Techniques:**
- WHS risk assessment tools (including psychosocial hazards)
- Remote work security assessment checklists
- Employee assistance programs (EAP) for cyber incident trauma
- Secure collaboration and communication tools

**Key Control Activities:**
- WHS policies addressing psychosocial hazards (including cyber)
- Remote work security standards and assessments
- Cyber safety and online harassment training
- Incident reporting and response for cyber-related safety issues
- Worker consultation on digital safety risks

### 3.6 Anti-Money Laundering / Counter-Terrorism Financing (AML/CTF) Act 2006

**What:** Legislation requiring reporting entities to implement AML/CTF programs, conduct customer due diligence, and report suspicious matters and threshold transactions. [web:42][web:89]

**Why:** Combats money laundering and terrorism financing by imposing obligations on financial institutions and designated services to detect and report suspicious activities.

**When to Use:**
- When operating as reporting entity under AML/CTF Act
- Designing AML/CTF compliance programs

## 9. Top 50 FAQs for GRC Consultants

### General GRC & Risk

**Q1: What is the difference between ISO 31000 and ISO 27001?**  
**A:** ISO 31000 provides principles and guidelines for enterprise risk management (not certifiable), while ISO 27001 specifies requirements for an Information Security Management System (certifiable). ISO 31000 is broader and can be used to manage all organizational risks, while ISO 27001 focuses specifically on information security risks. [web:50][web:56]

**Q2: How do I integrate ISO 31000 with ISO 27001?**  
**A:** Use ISO 31000 as the enterprise risk management framework, and align ISO 27001's risk assessment and treatment processes with ISO 31000's risk management process. This ensures information security risks are managed within the broader enterprise risk context.

**Q3: What is the OCEG Red Book used for?**  
**A:** The OCEG GRC Capability Model (Red Book) provides a comprehensive framework for building integrated GRC capability across Learn, Align, Perform, and Review components. It helps organizations reduce GRC silos and improve efficiency by integrating governance, risk, and compliance activities. [web:18]

**Q4: How does COBIT 2019 differ from ISO 27001?**  
**A:** COBIT 2019 focuses on IT governance and management (40 objectives across governance and management domains), while ISO 27001 focuses specifically on information security management. COBIT is broader (covers all IT governance), while ISO 27001 is deeper on security. They complement each other well. [web:57]

**Q5: Is AS 3806 still relevant given ISO 19600?**  
**A:** AS 3806 remains relevant in Australia, particularly for Australian-specific compliance programs. While ISO 19600 is the international compliance management standard, AS 3806 provides Australian-specific guidance and is often referenced by Australian regulators. Many organizations use both in combination. [web:54]

---

### Information Security & Privacy

**Q6: What are the key changes in ISO 27001:2022 vs 2013?**  
**A:** ISO 27001:2022 reorganized Annex A controls from 114 controls in 14 domains to 93 controls in 4 themes (Organizational, People, Physical, Technological). It also added 11 new controls (e.g., cloud security, data leakage, ICT supply chain) and aligned terminology with other ISO management system standards.

**Q7: Do I need ISO 27001 if I have NIST CSF?**  
**A:** It depends on requirements. ISO 27001 is certifiable and often required by customers and regulators. NIST CSF is flexible and excellent for cybersecurity program maturity. Many organizations implement both, using NIST CSF for cybersecurity operations and ISO 27001 for certifiable security management.

**Q8: What is the relationship between ISO 27001 and ISO 27701?**  
**A:** ISO 27701 is an extension to ISO 27001/27002, adding privacy-specific requirements and controls. Organizations can implement ISO 27001 (ISMS) and extend it to ISO 27701 (PIMS) for integrated security and privacy management. ISO 27701 supports Privacy Act and GDPR compliance.

**Q9: How does ISO 22301 complement ISO 27001?**  
**A:** ISO 22301 (BCMS) focuses on business continuity and resilience, while ISO 27001 (ISMS) focuses on information security. They share common management system structure (Annex SL) and can be integrated. ISO 22301 ensures continuity of operations during disruptions, including cyber incidents covered by ISO 27001.

**Q10: What are the penalties for Privacy Act breaches in Australia?**  
**A:** Under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024, maximum penalties for serious privacy breaches are up to $50 million, 3x benefit gained, or 30% of turnover during the breach period. This significantly increased from previous penalties and aligns more closely with GDPR. [web:64]

---

### Australian Laws & Regulations

**Q11: What are directors' cyber security duties under Corporations Act?**  
**A:** Directors must exercise care and diligence (Section 180(1)) and act in good faith (Section 181), which includes ensuring company has policies, procedures, and systems to protect against cyber security breaches. ASIC Chair has stated cyber attacks are "foreseeable risk" requiring board priority. Penalties can be up to $1.65M for individuals or 15 years imprisonment. [web:35]

**Q12: What is an "eligible data breach" under NDB scheme?**  
**A:** An eligible data breach occurs when: (1) unauthorized access, disclosure, or loss of personal information; (2) likely to result in serious harm to individuals; and (3) entity has not prevented risk via remedial action. APP entities must notify OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable. [web:65][web:81]

**Q13: When do automated decision-making transparency obligations apply?**  
**A:** Effective 10 December 2026, APP entities must include in privacy policy details about automated decision-making that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect individuals' rights or interests. This includes AI-enabled systems, rule-based tools, and automated assessment technologies using personal information. [web:59]

**Q14: What are APRA CPS 234 requirements for third parties?**  
**A:** CPS 234 requires APRA-regulated entities to manage information security risks from service providers, including material service providers. This includes contractual requirements, security assessments, and ongoing oversight. Regulated entities remain accountable for information security even when using third parties. [web:13]

**Q15: How does APRA CPS 230 differ from CPS 234?**  
**A:** CPS 230 (Operational Risk Management, effective 1 July 2025) covers broader operational risks including disruptions and service provider risks. CPS 234 (Information Security) focuses specifically on information security. CPS 230 requires maintaining critical operations through disruptions, while CPS 234 requires information security capability commensurate with threats. [web:31][web:69]

---

### Cybersecurity Control Frameworks

**Q16: Should I use NIST CSF 2.0 or CIS Controls v8?**  
**A:** Both are excellent. NIST CSF 2.0 is flexible and framework-agnostic, ideal for governance and maturity assessments. CIS Controls v8 is more prescriptive and actionable, ideal for foundational security (IG1 → IG2 → IG3). Many organizations use both: CIS for control implementation, NIST CSF for governance and reporting. [web:66][web:72]

**Q17: What are the CIS Implementation Groups (IG1, IG2, IG3)?**  
**A:** CIS Controls v8 are organized into three Implementation Groups:
- **IG1:** Basic cyber hygiene (essential for all organizations)
- **IG2:** Enterprise controls (for organizations managing enterprise risk)
- **IG3:** Advanced controls (for high-security environments)
Organizations should start with IG1 and progress based on risk and maturity.

**Q18: How does NIST 800-53 relate to NIST CSF?**  
**A:** NIST 800-53 provides detailed security and privacy controls (20 families, 1000+ controls), while NIST CSF provides high-level functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover). NIST CSF Informative References map to NIST 800-53. Use NIST CSF for governance and NIST 800-53 for detailed control implementation. [web:68]

**Q19: What are the key changes in PCI DSS v4.0?**  
**A:** Key changes in PCI DSS v4.0 include: (1) Customized approach (risk-based control selection), (2) Enhanced authentication (MFA, phishing resistance), (3) Increased security awareness and phishing focus, (4) Expanded testing and monitoring requirements, (5) More flexibility in control implementation.

**Q20: How do I map ISO 27001 to NIST CSF 2.0?**  
**A:** Use NIST CSF 2.0 Informative References, which provide mappings to ISO 27001:2022. Additionally, research datasets provide detailed mappings between ISO 27001/27002, NIST CSF 2.0, and CIS Controls v8 (equivalents, subsets, supersets). [web:68][web:72]

### AI Governance & AI GRC

**Q21: What is ISO 42001 and do I need it?**  
**A:** ISO 42001 is the first certifiable AI management system standard, specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, and improving an AIMS. It's valuable for organizations developing, providing, or using AI systems, especially if seeking certification or demonstrating responsible AI practices. It complements ISO 27001 and ISO 31000 with AI-specific controls. [web:76]

**Q22: How does EU AI Act affect Australian companies?**  
**A:** EU AI Act has extraterritorial scope: Australian companies providing AI systems or services to EU market must comply. High-risk AI systems require conformity assessments, transparency, human oversight, and other obligations. Australian companies should assess AI systems for EU applicability and consider compliance even if not directly subject. [web:77]

**Q23: What are the four functions of NIST AI RMF?**  
**A:** NIST AI RMF includes four core functions: [web:74][web:75][web:80]
- **GOVERN:** Establish AI governance, policies, roles, accountability
- **MAP:** Understand context, categorize AI systems, identify risks
- **MEASURE:** Assess, evaluate, and track AI risks and performance
- **MANAGE:** Prioritize, treat, and monitor AI risks; maximize benefits

**Q24: How do I integrate AI governance with existing GRC?**  
**A:** Integrate AI governance with existing GRC by: (1) Adding AI risks to enterprise risk management (ISO 31000), (2) Extending ISMS (ISO 27001) with AI-specific controls (ISO 42001), (3) Including AI in operational risk (APRA CPS 230), (4) Using NIST AI RMF GOVERN function for AI governance structure, (5) Aligning with Privacy Act automated decision-making obligations.

**Q25: What are Australia's AI Ethics Principles?**  
**A:** Australia's AI Ethics Principles include 8 principles: human wellbeing, human-centred values, fairness, privacy protection and security, reliability and safety, transparency and explainability, contestability, and accountability. These were updated in 2025 to 6 essential practices for AI adoption guidance. [web:79]

### Sector-Specific & Compliance

**Q26: What are APRA's expectations for AI governance?**  
**A:** APRA expects regulated entities to include AI systems in operational risk management (CPS 230), apply information security controls (CPS 234), ensure board oversight of AI risks, manage third-party AI risks, and maintain resilience for AI-dependent operations. AI governance should align with ISO 42001 and NIST AI RMF principles.

**Q27: How do I comply with Spam Act and Do Not Call Register?**  
**A:** For Spam Act: obtain consent (express or inferred), include sender identification, and provide functional unsubscribe facility for all commercial electronic messages. For Do Not Call Register: check register before making telemarketing calls and do not call registered numbers (unless consent given). ACMA enforces both with significant penalties. [web:83][web:86]

**Q28: What are AML/CTF Act obligations for reporting entities?**  
**A:** Reporting entities must implement AML/CTF program (Part A: risk assessment, policies, controls; Part B: customer due diligence), conduct customer identification and verification, ongoing monitoring, and report suspicious matters (SMRs), threshold transactions (TTRs), and international funds transfers (IFTIs). Records must be retained for 7 years. [web:42][web:89]

**Q29: What OAIC resources are available for privacy compliance?**  
**A:** OAIC provides APP Guidelines, Data Breach Preparation and Response Guide, Privacy Management Framework, and conducts compliance sweeps (2026 sweep targeting privacy policies). OAIC also provides guidance on automated decision-making transparency and regulatory action guidelines. [web:81][web:82][web:85]

**Q30: How do I comply with PSPF for government contracts?**  
**A:** PSPF requires non-corporate Commonwealth entities (and often contractors) to implement security across six domains (Governance, People, Information, Physical, Cyber, Security Assurance). Annual self-assessment and reporting is required. Align with ISM for cyber security controls and engage with National Security SMEs for classified information. [web:39][web:71]

### Practical Implementation

**Q31: What is the best way to start a GRC program?**  
**A:** Start with: (1) Assess current state (maturity, gaps, risks), (2) Define scope and objectives (what frameworks, regulations apply), (3) Establish governance and accountability (charter, board oversight), (4) Implement foundational controls (ISO 27001, CIS IG1, Privacy Act), (5) Build roadmap for maturity progression, (6) Integrate with enterprise risk management (ISO 31000).

**Q32: How do I prioritize multiple frameworks and regulations?**  
**A:** Prioritize based on: (1) Regulatory requirements (mandatory: Privacy Act, APRA, ASIC, etc.), (2) Business requirements (customer contracts, certifications), (3) Risk profile (highest risks first), (4) Framework harmonization (use mappings to implement once, comply multiple), (5) Maturity progression (foundational → advanced).

**Q33: What tools are best for GRC program management?**  
**A:** Common GRC platforms include: ServiceNow GRC, MetricStream, OneTrust (privacy, security, GRC), Archer, LogicGate, Hyperproof. For smaller organizations: spreadsheets, Jira, Confluence, or specialized tools for specific frameworks (ISO 27001 toolkits, NIST CSF assessment tools).

**Q34: How do I measure GRC program effectiveness?**  
**A:** Measure using: (1) KPIs (control effectiveness, compliance rates, incident metrics), (2) Maturity assessments (framework maturity scores), (3) Risk metrics (risk reduction, KRIs), (4) Audit and assessment results (findings, remediation), (5) Board and stakeholder reporting (dashboards, scorecards).

**Q35: What is the best approach for control mapping?**  
**A:** Use existing mappings where available (NIST CSF Informative References, CIS to NIST CSF, ISO 27001 to NIST CSF). Implement controls once and map to multiple frameworks. Use GRC tools with framework libraries and mappings. Maintain mapping evidence and update as frameworks evolve.

### Certification & Audits

**Q36: Is ISO 31000 certifiable?**  
**A:** No. ISO 31000 provides guidelines, not requirements, and is not intended for certification. However, it provides a foundation for certifiable risk management systems (e.g., ISO 27001 risk assessment, ISO 22301 BCMS risk assessment). [web:50][web:56]

**Q37: Is ISO 27001 certification worth it?**  
**A:** Yes, for many organizations. Benefits include: (1) Customer requirements (especially enterprise and government), (2) Competitive advantage, (3) Improved security posture, (4) Regulatory compliance (e.g., Privacy Act APP 11), (5) Vendor security assessments. Costs include implementation, audits, and maintenance.

**Q38: How long does ISO 27001 certification take?**  
**A:** Typically 6-18 months depending on organization size, complexity, and current maturity. Process includes: (1) Gap analysis and scoping, (2) Risk assessment and treatment, (3) Control implementation, (4) Internal audit and management review, (5) Stage 1 and Stage 2 certification audits, (6) Surveillance audits (annual) and recertification (3 years).

**Q39: What is the difference between Stage 1 and Stage 2 ISO 27001 audits?**  
**A:** Stage 1: Documentation review (ISMS documentation, scope, risk assessment, SoA). Stage 2: Implementation audit (control testing, interviews, evidence review). Both conducted by certification body. Stage 1 identifies gaps; Stage 2 determines certification recommendation.

**Q40: How often do ISO 27001 audits occur?**  
**A:** Initial certification: Stage 1 and Stage 2 audits. Surveillance audits: Annual (years 1 and 2). Recertification audit: Year 3 (full audit, similar to initial Stage 2). Organizations must maintain ISMS and undergo continuous monitoring.

### Incident Response & Breaches

**Q41: What are my obligations after a data breach under NDB scheme?**  
**A:** Assess whether breach is "eligible" (unauthorized access/disclosure, likely serious harm, not remediated). If eligible: notify OAIC using NDB form, notify affected individuals (email, phone, website), include entity details, breach description, information kinds, and recommendations. Do this as soon as practicable. [web:65][web:81]

**Q42: How do I prepare for data breaches?**  
**A:** Establish data breach response plan and team, implement breach detection and monitoring, prepare breach assessment workflows, develop NDB notification templates, conduct tabletop exercises, maintain breach register, and coordinate with legal, IT, security, and communications teams.
